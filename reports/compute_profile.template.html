At the batch size we train with, roughly a third of every step is fixed overhead — kernel launch, sync, Python dispatch — that faster silicon does not touch. Raising the batch size costs nothing in hardware and lifts the ceiling on any upgrade from 3.6× to 43×. That is worth more than the GPU generation gap at this batch size.

TamIA is worth moving to, but for a smaller and more specific reason than it first appears. The cluster has ~260 GPUs against your three — yet a Bayesian sweep cannot use more than ~8–16 trials in parallel before it stops being Bayesian, so the realistic gain is 4–8×, not 80×. And the thing actually preventing the move is not compute: wandb agent requires internet, and TamIA's compute nodes have none.

Why 3.6× and 2.6× are consistent

The 3.6× ceiling assumes an infinitely fast GPU, which only removes the 72% of the step that is variable. The H200's projected 6.25× on that portion already captures most of the available headroom — which is precisely why more silicon is the wrong lever here.

Recommended order

  • Replace the sweep mechanism — Optuna with filesystem storage. This is the gating item; nothing else matters until it is solved.
  • Move to batch ~1024 and 16 dataloader workers, and re-tune the learning rate. Worth more than the hardware change (3.4× vs 2.3× per-GPU gain on H100).
  • Keep rabelais for development — zero queue wait and a 13-minute grid beat any shared cluster for iteration.
  • Request 1–2 nodes on gpubase_bynode_b2 (12 h) for the sweep; bundle many trials per job, four concurrent — one per GPU, never split.

Section 2

What actually limits it

Step time is almost perfectly linear in batch size — R² = 0.999 for both phases. The intercept is per-step cost that does not shrink with batch size, and faster silicon does not remove it.

full    step_ms = 15.8 + 0.1618 × batch
frozen  step_ms =  7.2 + 0.0655 × batch
Three measurements agree this is not compute-bound

bf16 made it 8% slower, not faster (52.7 → 57.1 ms) — autocast overhead exceeds any tensor-core gain when kernels are this small. TF32 did help (+16%), so a modest matmul component exists. Memory is nowhere near a limit: batch 4096 peaks at 21.8 GB of 48, and the grid used 1.5 GB. 4,487 kernel launches per step for 256 molecules — the signature of many tiny operations, which is what a GNN over ~25-node graphs produces.

The cross-check that validates the model

The GPU-only step time predicts the grid's observed end-to-end epoch to +11% (53.6 s predicted vs 59.4 s observed). That gap is the CPU pipeline not perfectly overlapping the GPU — the expected direction and magnitude. The benchmark and the real runs are measuring the same thing.

Section 3

Sustained load: capped, but the trend is unmeasured

Under load the cards ran at 1,500–1,800 MHz against a 2,100 MHz max boost — 14–29% down — at 84–88 °C and 260–287 W of a 300 W limit. The clocks_event_reasons field asserted 0x4 (SW power cap) and 0x20 (SW thermal slowdown), often together. The throttling is real and identified, not inferred.

What this does not show

A trend. The sawtooth is the four job waves — the recoveries at ~3.5 and ~7 minutes are the idle gaps between them — so a linear slope fitted over this 13-minute window is confounded by the scheduling pattern, not evidence of thermal decay. A naive fit gives −6 to −18 MHz/min; that number should not be used. Settling it needs one sustained run of an hour or more. It is deliberately excluded from the projection.

Caveat on batch size, and it is not small

Larger batches are a free compute win and not a free training change: at batch 1024 an epoch has a quarter as many optimiser steps, which generally needs the learning rate re-tuned. Treat it as a change requiring a re-run of the schedule comparison, not a drop-in.

Section 4.5

Should you split a GPU across concurrent runs?

rabelais permits splitting one GPU across concurrent jobs (--gres=mps:N) at the cost of the other two; TamIA's whole-node rule raises the same question. Measured directly — N processes pinned to one GPU with a synchronised start, timing the real training step on a device-resident batch.

Memory would have permitted 8 concurrent processes at batch 1024 (47.4 GB of 49 GB). Sizing by memory alone would have suggested 8 splits and lost a third of the throughput — the error this measurement exists to prevent. Since splitting costs the other two GPUs, the ranking is unambiguous: three whole GPUs give ~17,500 mol/s against ~5,280 for any split configuration.

A prediction this refuted

Before measuring, the expectation was that batch 256 would gain from splitting — 28% of its step is fixed overhead, so concurrent processes should fill the idle gaps. It did not. The reasoning failed because with no MPS daemon, processes time-slice: one CUDA context runs at a time, so idle gaps cannot be filled however much idle exists. This measurement therefore cannot distinguish “the GPU is saturated” from “time-slicing prevents overlap.” Under a real MPS daemon the answer may differ — untested.

Section 5

What TamIA would buy

The projection scales each part of the step by the ratio appropriate to it. The fixed 15.8 ms/step scales ×1 — launch and dispatch overhead is host-side. The variable 0.1618 ms/molecule scales by a ratio bounded on both sides:

  • Conservative, 1.73× — if the variable part is compute-bound, scaling with fp32 throughput.
  • Optimistic, 4.36× (H100) / 6.25× (H200) — if bandwidth-bound, scaling the measured 687 GB/s against 3.35 / 4.8 TB/s at the same 89% efficiency.

The evidence points nearer the bandwidth end — bf16 slower, thousands of tiny kernels — but TF32's +16% shows a real compute component, so the honest answer is a range, not a number.

Two arguments the measurements do not support

Rejected: “the H200's 141 GB unlocks bigger batches”

It does not. The workload peaks at 1.5 GB at the batch we train with and 21.8 GB at batch 4096 — on a 48 GB card that is already not full. Throughput saturates long before memory does. The H200's headline spec is its least relevant one here.

Conditional: “NVLink will speed this up”

Independent single-GPU runs exchange nothing — no gradient all-reduce, no model sharding — so NVLink is irrelevant to them. But TamIA requires a job to use all 4 GPUs on its node, forcing a choice: pack four independent runs one-per-GPU (NVLink stays irrelevant, and the concurrency measurements say this is right), or data-parallel a single run across four (NVLink then matters). Pick the former.

So the cluster is far larger than claimed — and the binding constraint is not the hardware. Bayesian optimisation is sequential by construction: its advantage over random search is choosing each trial conditioned on completed ones. Run k concurrently and all k come from the same stale posterior. Past roughly 8–16 parallel trials it degenerates toward random search with extra machinery. The realistic allocation is 1–2 nodes.

The actual blocker is not compute

A wandb sweep cannot run on TamIA as currently designed. python -m wandb agent must reach the wandb server to fetch each next configuration, and TamIA's compute nodes have no internet. This gates the sweep regardless of hardware. The fix is an optimiser whose controller lives on the shared filesystem — Optuna with SQLite/PostgreSQL storage gives TPE and runs fully offline, with WANDB_MODE=offline and a wandb sync from a login node afterwards.

A real 4–8×, from parallelism plus a ~3.4× per-GPU gain at batch 1024 — not the 80× the device count suggests. Queue wait is unmeasured and additive. Two things remain unknown and bear directly on these numbers: your allocation share (sinfo shows the whole partition) and queue wait.

Section 6

Open questions

  • Sustained-load throttling. Confirmed present, but its trajectory is unmeasured — 13 minutes of sawtooth load cannot separate decay from scheduling. Needs one sustained run of ≥1 hour. Excluded from the projection.
  • Where in the 1.73×–6.25× range the variable part sits. Settling it needs a memory-throughput profile of the actual GNN kernels (Nsight Compute), not just the microbenchmark bounds used here.
  • Whether TamIA behaves as projected. src/benchmark.py is self-contained and runs there unchanged; one execution replaces this whole section with measurements.

Every figure traces to reports/benchmark.json, grid_summary.json, grid_timings.csv or gpu_telemetry.csv in the finetune_minimol repository. src/benchmark.py refuses to start if another process is resident on the GPU — every timing would otherwise measure contention — and keeps GPU-only, CPU-only and end-to-end timings strictly separate, because conflating them is how a compute report ends up recommending the wrong hardware.
